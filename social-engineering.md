# Social Engineering in Cybersecurity

## Introduction

Social engineering is the use of deception, manipulation, or psychological pressure to trick people into revealing sensitive information, performing actions, or giving unauthorized access to systems.

Instead of relying only on technical vulnerabilities, attackers exploit human trust, fear, curiosity, or urgency.

## Common Types of Social Engineering

### 1. Phishing

Phishing involves fraudulent messages designed to trick people into revealing information, clicking malicious links, or downloading harmful files.

**Example:** A victim receives a fake bank email asking them to verify their account through a fraudulent website.

**Prevention:**
- Verify the sender's address.
- Avoid suspicious links and attachments.
- Visit official websites directly.
- Use multi-factor authentication.

### 2. Pretexting

Pretexting occurs when an attacker invents a believable story or identity to obtain information or persuade someone to take an action.

**Example:** Someone pretends to be an IT support employee and asks a worker for their password.

**Prevention:**
- Verify identities through trusted channels.
- Follow organizational security procedures.
- Never share passwords with supposed support staff.

### 3. Baiting

Baiting uses something tempting to persuade a victim to take an unsafe action.

**Example:** An attacker leaves a USB drive labelled "Confidential Salaries" where someone might find it, hoping they will connect it to a computer.

**Prevention:**
- Never connect unknown USB devices.
- Submit suspicious devices to authorized IT or security personnel.
- Avoid downloading unknown software.

### 4. Quid Pro Quo

Quid pro quo involves offering a supposed benefit or service in exchange for information or an action.

**Example:** A scammer promises technical assistance in exchange for a user's login credentials.

**Prevention:**
- Use verified support channels.
- Never exchange passwords for assistance.
- Confirm unusual requests before acting.

### 5. Tailgating

Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted physical location without proper authorization.

**Example:** Someone follows an employee through a secure office entrance without using their own access credentials.

**Prevention:**
- Do not allow unknown people to bypass access controls.
- Follow visitor verification procedures.
- Report suspicious entry attempts.

## Recognizing Social Engineering

Warning signs include:
- Unexpected requests for sensitive information.
- Messages creating extreme urgency or fear.
- Offers that seem too good to be true.
- Unusual payment or credential requests.
- Requests to bypass normal security procedures.

## Why Social Engineering Matters

Even well-protected systems can be compromised if an attacker successfully manipulates an authorized user.

Cybersecurity awareness, identity verification, and appropriate technical controls help reduce these risks.

## Conclusion

Social engineering targets human behaviour to gain information, access, or other advantages.

Recognizing manipulation and verifying unusual requests are important parts of cybersecurity.
